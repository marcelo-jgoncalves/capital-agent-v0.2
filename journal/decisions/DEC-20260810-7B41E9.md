## Opportunity

A practical, plain-language PT-BR guide/checklist for solo developers and
small teams on AWS's July 2025 Free Tier overhaul (12-month free usage →
6-month, $100-200 expiring-credit model): how to tell which model your
account is on, how to set up budget alerts and usage monitoring so credit
expiration doesn't turn into a surprise bill, and what to do when credits
run out. Purely technical/organizational content — no tax, legal, or
regulatory classification involved, unlike the NFS-e candidate
(`DEC-20260810-C5EA4F`) that is currently on standby specifically over
domain-confidence risk in a compliance-adjacent topic.

## Mechanism of return

Same low-labor pattern validated in method (not yet in market) by the
NFS-e research cycle: identify a live, dated, still-current source of
confusion; produce a careful, accurate, narrow guide; sell cheaply on a
Brazilian infoproduct platform (Kiwify, per earlier research) or, as an
alternative/complementary channel unique to this candidate, publish as an
article on the human owner's own existing property (`iadecifrada.com.br`,
whose stated positioning is exactly "da AWS à OpenAI" AWS/cloud content) as
a free lead-magnet/authority piece, optionally with a paid, more complete
checklist/template upsell.

## Evidence

- `WebSearch`, 2026-08-10: confirmed AWS replaced the Free Tier structure
  on 2025-07-15 for new accounts (credit-based, 6-month or credit-exhaustion
  cutoff, automatic account closure after) while legacy (pre-2025-07-15)
  accounts keep the old 12-month model — two co-existing regimes, a
  concrete source of "which rules apply to me" confusion by construction.
  Independent English-language source (infratally.com) corroborates: "many
  articles still describe this model, even though it no longer applies to
  new AWS accounts" and developers report being "blindsided by AWS
  billing" because "AWS changed it and didn't exactly shout about it."
- Competitive check: the topic is heavily crowded in English (Rackspace,
  Tech Insider Australia, oneuptime, cloudtoggle, cloudwebschool,
  infratally all published 2026 coverage). In Portuguese, only one
  competing piece surfaced (`unicast.com.br`, "Adeus Free Tier de 12
  meses!"). `WebFetch` on that article found it accurate and covers the
  policy change itself well, but explicitly leaves gaps this candidate
  would fill: no usage-monitoring/budget-alert guidance, no concrete
  cost/service scenarios, no actionable steps for what to do as credits
  approach expiration — it stops at "here's what changed," not "here's what
  to do about it." Audience skews beginner/career-changer, leaving small
  teams running real (if small) workloads underserved.
- Method check against the two failure patterns logged this cycle
  (`context/knowledge/rejected-opportunities.md`,
  `context/knowledge/recurring-errors.md`): this is not a generic listicle
  idea (sourced from a specific, dated policy change), and — unlike the
  three non-regulatory candidates rejected earlier today (Microsoft
  Publisher EOL, Mercado Livre fee changes, WhatsApp Business API pricing,
  all crowded within days by vendor/agency SEO content) — the PT-BR gap
  here survived a direct crowding check specifically because the existing
  PT-BR coverage is thin (one article) despite the topic being significant,
  likely because it requires reconciling AWS's own inconsistent messaging
  and stale documentation between two co-existing account regimes, which is
  exactly the "genuinely hard to correctly interpret from primary sources"
  moat identified as the differentiator in `recurring-errors.md`.

## Why this could fail

- The English-language coverage is extensive; if a Brazilian reader is
  comfortable reading English sources or auto-translating them, the PT-BR
  gap matters less than the raw search-result count suggests. Not yet
  tested whether PT-BR-only searchers actually convert.
- AWS could further change Free Tier terms again, requiring the guide to be
  kept current — same staleness risk noted for the NFS-e candidate, same
  mitigation (state a "verified as of [date]" framing, cite official AWS
  docs, avoid asserting specifics that change quietly).
- Audience (developers/small tech teams) may be more price-sensitive about
  paying for a guide than about paying an accountant-adjacent product like
  the NFS-e guide was targeting — developers are used to finding this kind
  of information free (Stack Overflow, Reddit, official docs), which could
  cap willingness to pay even where the PT-BR content gap is real. This is
  a weaker demand-validation risk than the crowding risk and would need a
  cheap validation step regardless of channel chosen.
- Distributing via `iadecifrada.com.br` (the human owner's own property) is
  free of marketplace fees but was not evaluated for legal/reputational
  gate implications; needs an explicit check against `CRITICAL_DECISIONS.md`
  before it's treated as the default channel, same as the marketplace
  option would need.

## Alternatives considered

- **Do nothing / keep searching**: rejected as the sole path for the same
  reason as the NFS-e cycle — this is the first non-regulatory candidate
  today whose PT-BR crowding check came back genuinely thin, after three
  that didn't (`context/knowledge/rejected-opportunities.md`).
- **A broader "AWS cost optimization" guide** (not scoped to the Free Tier
  change specifically): rejected for this first pass — broader framing
  would compete directly with the well-established, heavily-covered
  "reduce your AWS bill" genre; the narrow, dated-event framing is what
  produced the thin-competition result here.
- **Selling exclusively via Kiwify/marketplace** vs. **publishing via the
  human owner's own blog**: not yet decided; both remain open pending
  further scoping (see Decision below) — the existing-property option is
  new information specific to this candidate (the NFS-e guide had no
  equivalent existing-authority channel) and needs its own evaluation
  rather than defaulting to the marketplace pattern already used once.

- Base case: an accurate, actionable PT-BR guide fills a real, verified

## Addendum 1 (2026-08-10T17:50:00-03:00) — Primary-source verification complete; found a genuine two-page AWS documentation conflict

Verified directly against AWS's own official documentation
(`docs.aws.amazon.com/awsaccountbilling`, plus `aws.amazon.com/free` and
`aws.amazon.com/free/legacy/free-tier-faqs`), not secondary blogs:

- **Free plan (accounts created on/after 2025-07-15)**: USD $100 signup
  credit + up to $100 more from onboarding activities (USD $200 total),
  valid 6 months or until credits are exhausted, whichever comes first.
  30+ "Always Free" services remain available regardless of credit status,
  on both Free and Paid plans.
- **Legacy accounts (created before 2025-07-15)**: keep the original
  12-month free tier with fixed per-service monthly allowances (e.g. 5 GB
  S3 standard storage, 750 EC2 t2/t3.micro instance-hours/month); usage
  beyond the limit is simply billed at standard rates, and unused allowance
  does not roll over.
- **What actually happens when the Free plan's 6 months/credits run out**
  (the guide's core actionable content, and the gap the competing PT-BR
  article does not cover): the account **closes automatically**, access to
  resources and data is lost, AWS retains content for 90 days before
  **permanent deletion** of the account and all its resources, and the only
  way to keep access is upgrading to a Paid plan within that 90-day window
  (any unused credits carry over automatically to the resulting bill).
  AWS sends email warnings at 50%/25%/10% remaining credits and at
  15/7/2 days before the 6-month deadline.
- **A genuine documentation conflict worth calling out explicitly in the
  guide**: AWS's own `avoid-charges-after-free-tier.html` page describes a
  softer, generic framing ("after your eligibility expires, you're charged
  at standard AWS billing rates for usage") that does not mention automatic
  account closure or data loss at all, while the more specific, more
  current `free-tier-plans.html` page states plainly that the **Free**
  account plan (as opposed to Paid) "closes automatically, and you lose
  access to your resources and data" — a materially different consequence.
  Read together, the first page appears to describe the Paid-plan /
  legacy-style "you just get billed" outcome, while the second describes
  the Free-plan-specific "you lose your data" outcome, and AWS does not
  cross-reference the two to make that distinction obvious. This is exactly
  the kind of primary-source reconciliation gap the recurring-errors
  finding (`context/knowledge/recurring-errors.md`) predicted as the real
  differentiator — a reader relying on the first page alone (a very
  findable, generically-titled page) would wrongly conclude they're safe
  from losing data on the Free plan.

No material conflict found with the earlier secondary-source evidence; the
verification instead *added* a finding (the two-page conflict above) worth
making the guide's central, differentiating point rather than a footnote.

## Addendum 6 (2026-08-10T18:35:00-03:00) — Self-correction: email capture has its own LGPD obligation, missed when recommending the mechanism

Human owner caught a gap the agent should have flagged at Addendum 5: the
AdSense rejection reasoning stated this candidate avoids "the compliance
dimension" the standing-by NFS-e candidate carries. That framing was
**incomplete, not wrong about ads specifically** — declining AdSense does
remove the cookie-consent trigger, but collecting email addresses is
itself personal-data processing under the LGPD, regardless of ads. This
should have been named when the email-capture mechanism was first
recommended (Addendum 4/5), not only after the human owner raised it.

Minimum practical requirements identified for a simple lead-capture form
(not a full legal compliance audit — same "organize what's already known,
don't assert unverified specifics" discipline as the page's own content):

- Explicit, non-pre-checked opt-in consent tied to a stated purpose
  (legal basis = consent, LGPD Art. 7, I).
- A short, visible privacy notice: what's collected, why, who's
  responsible, how to request deletion.
- Purpose limitation — the email list is not repurposed without new
  consent.
- An easy opt-out/deletion path.

Recommendation to minimize implementation and security burden: use a
mainstream email-tool provider with a free tier that already handles
double opt-in and unsubscribe natively (Brevo or MailerLite suggested) —
rather than a custom/DIY collection mechanism where the security and
retention obligations would fall entirely on the human owner to manage.

Updated `experiments/drafts/aws-free-tier-guide-draft-v1.md`: replaced the
placeholder comment with the actual consent-checkbox copy, a short privacy
notice, and an implementation note listing what's still a placeholder
(tool choice, contact email for deletion requests) before this can be
published.

**Process note, not just a content fix**: log this as a lesson — when
recommending a data-collection mechanism (even something as small as an
email field), name its own compliance surface at the point of
recommendation, not only if asked. Candidate for
`context/knowledge/recurring-errors.md` if this pattern repeats (currently
a single occurrence, so not logged there yet per that file's own
two-occurrence threshold).
